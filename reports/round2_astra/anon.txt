CORRELATIONS OF ARTIN STATUS AMONG PRIMES:
CONSECUTIVE-PRIME ANTICORRELATION, CROSS-BASE
ENTANGLEMENT,
AND AN ELEMENTARY DECOMPOSITION
Abstract. Say a prime p is Artin base a if a is a primitive root modulo p. This paper studies
correlations of Artin status along two axes — consecutive primes at a fixed base, and several
bases at a fixed prime — for all 50,847,531 primes up to 109 , and then asks what, if anything,
such correlations are computationally worth.
Along the first axis, Artin statuses of consecutive primes are measurably anticorrelated
at base 10: P (Artn+1 | Artn ) = 0.36514 against P (Artn+1 | ¬Artn ) = 0.37928, a deficit
δ = −0.01414 (Pearson 10,167 on one degree of freedom; signed square root −100.8 under
a nominal independent-status reference, over 50,847,530 pairs). The dependence has strong
gap structure and contains one deterministic component, an elementary exclusion law : if
p, p + g > 5 are prime with g ≡ 20 (mod 40), then 10 is a quadratic residue modulo exactly one
of them, so the two can never both be Artin; among 2,195,882 pairs at gaps 20 and 60 there
is no doubly-Artin pair, and an independent recomputation extends this to 194,296,748 such
pairs below 1011 , again with none. Conversely g ≡ 0 (mod 40) forces equal quadratic-residue
status and strong positive dependence (P (Artn+1 | Artn ) = 0.899 at g = 40). Conditioning
on the joint residues of (pn , pn+1 ) modulo 120 or 840 removes 93% and 97% respectively of
a selected-cell signed residual association. As an intermediate link we report the apparently
unreported anticorrelation r(ω(pn − 1), ω(pn+1 − 1)) = −0.041.
Along the second axis we record a second exclusion law: writing d = sqf(ab) for the
squarefree part of ab, no prime with dp = −1 is simultaneously Artin for a and for b; whenever
a and b have different squarefree parts this bars half of all primes, and it implies a triple form
— if sqf(c) = sqf(ab) then no odd prime is Artin for a, b, c at once (e.g. 2, 5, 10). Across the 66
base pairs of {2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 10, 11, 13, 15, 17, 21, 29} the measured same-prime correlation ϕ(a, b)
is positive for 64 pairs (mean 0.0352); conditioning on ω(p − 1) removes only 55% of it, while
conditioning on a fine signature of p − 1 removes 95%. The residual is concentrated on the three
pairs whose product class completes a triple and lies in the quadratic-character channel. Joint
densities predicted by the Matthews–Moree–Stevenhagen Kummer-degree heuristic match the
measured densities to a mean relative error of 0.012% with no fitted parameters.
Finally we audit the computational content of these correlations. Measured by cross-entropy,
the consecutive-prime correlation carries 0.000009 nats (1.3×10−5 bits) of predictive information
about the next prime’s Artin status beyond the elementary joint-residue baseline, which itself
carries 0.4195 nats. Both exclusion laws are computationally redundant: each follows from the
multiplicativity of quadratic characters. The one genuinely usable filter — a primitive root
must be a quadratic non-residue — is elementary, is exactly what the observed conductor-40
channel expresses, and halves the factorizations of p − 1 in a scanning search; ordering the
small prime channels of p − 1 before the full factorization adds a further 15%. We conclude
that these correlations describe arithmetic structure rather than supply algorithms, and we
state precisely what would have to change for that verdict to change.

Three further strands meet here. The first is the bias discovered numerically by Knapowski–
Turán, Ko and Ash–Beltis–Gross–Sinnott and quantitatively treated by Lemke Oliver and
Soundararajan [14]: consecutive primes in the same reduced residue class modulo q occur
markedly less often than equidistribution predicts, a phenomenon explained conjecturally by
the Hardy–Littlewood k-tuple conjectures [9]. Since the Artin property of p is governed by the
factorisation of p − 1, and since residues of consecutive primes modulo small q are correlated
by [14], one expects some induced dependence between the Artin indicators of consecutive
primes. The second is the joint behaviour across bases at a fixed prime, computed under GRH
by Matthews [24] and systematically by Moree–Stevenhagen [23], with the qualitative message
that Aa and Ab are not independent and that the deviation is governed by entanglement
of the associated Kummer–Galois extensions; the framework remains active (see [20] and
references there). The third is the question of which primes at a fixed shift can both be Artin:
Tinková–Waxman–Zindulka [13] identify shift–base pairs admitting no such prime, and our
Theorem 2 is the base-10 instance of that phenomenon; we claim no priority for it.
The present paper asks the statistical question along both axes — how Artin statuses
of consecutive primes are correlated on average, and how Artin statuses of several bases
are correlated at the same prime — and then asks a question we have not seen posed in this
literature: what is such a correlation computationally worth? 1 The answer we give is a measured
negative, and we regard it as part of the result rather than a failure of it.
Two comparisons should be made explicit. Garcia–Kahoro–Luca [6] (see also [7]) compare
the number of primitive roots, φ(p − 1) versus φ(p + 1), for twin pairs; Pollack [17] and
Baker–Pollack [2] prove existence of runs of consecutive primes each having a prescribed
primitive root. Those are existence results, produced by sieves that select rare favourable
configurations; they say nothing about the typical joint behaviour along the sequence of primes.
The questions studied here are complementary: the average correlation, its decomposition, and
its computational content.

The condition dp = −1 is a condition on residue classes. For d > 1 the character d· is
non-principal, of fundamental discriminant d if d ≡ 1 (mod 4) and 4d otherwise; either way
the discriminant divides 4d, so the excluded set is an explicit union of reduced classes modulo
4d, and exactly half of those classes carry character −1. By the prime number theorem in
arithmetic progressions the excluded set then has density exactly 1/2 among the odd primes.
So for any two bases with d > 1 — no third base required, no special configuration — half of all
primes are deterministically barred from being Artin for both, and Theorem 6 is a congruence
exclusion law.

Corollary 8. Within any multiplicative triple — for instance (2, 5, 10), (3, 5, 15), (3, 7, 21),
(2, 3, 6) — an odd prime can be Artin for at most two of the three bases. The restriction to odd
p is necessary and not cosmetic: every odd integer is vacuously a primitive root modulo 2, so
all three members of (3, 5, 15) are Artin base 2.
The gain in passing from Theorem 7 to Theorem 6 is not cosmetic, though it is a gain in
usable content rather than in logical strength: both are pointwise statements at every odd
prime. The difference is in what one must know to apply them. To invoke Theorem 7 one must
first identify a third base c with sqf(c) = sqf(ab) and establish that c is a primitive root at the
prime in question — itself an instance of the Artin problem, conjectural in general and (under
GRH) of positive but base-dependent density. Theorem 6 needs neither: the barring condition
is a congruence, decidable from p alone by reciprocity, and for d > 1 it holds on a set of density
exactly 1/2. The triple law is the shadow that the pair law casts when one insists on reading
the barring character off a primitive root instead of off a residue class.
Remark 9 (Sharpness of the hypotheses). In both theorems no hypothesis on the bases
beyond positivity is needed, only “p odd” (together with the barring condition (d/p) = −1 in
Theorem 6). Non-squareness of the bases is not required: if a is a square then χa (p) = +1 and
a is not a primitive root, so the conclusion is immediate. The same remark disposes of c = 1
in Theorem 7, which arises whenever sqf(a) = sqf(b). The hypothesis p ̸= 2 must however be
kept, and is not removable: (Z/2Z)× is trivial, so every odd integer is vacuously a primitive
root mod 2 and e.g. (a, b, c) = (3, 5, 15) violates the conclusion there. The accompanying Lean
formalisation (Section 5) confirms independently that the a ̸≡ 0 and b ̸≡ 0 (mod p) assumptions
are never used.

Remark 11. Theorem 6 is the same-prime analogue of the gap exclusion law for consecutive
primes proved in [18]: both are instances of a parity obstruction in the group generated by

obstruction is arranged by multiplicative dependence. The Galois-theoretic density computations

recalled in Section 1 (the degree of the compositum of the relevant Kummer extensions drops);
the elementary pointwise form seems worth recording.
6. Data and computation
The two axes of this paper use two censuses over the same primes, with slightly different
exclusions at the low end, by design. We describe each.
The consecutive-prime census (base 10). For every prime 7 ≤ p ≤ 109 we compute: the
gap to the neighbouring primes, the number ω(p − 1) of distinct prime factors of p − 1 (by
trial division), the Artin indicator (computed exactly via 10(p−1)/q ̸≡ 1 (mod p) for each prime
q | p − 1, using GMP modular exponentiation), and small residues of p. A segmented sieve of
Eratosthenes generates the primes; the corrected sieve emits all 50,847,531 primes from 7 to
999,999,937 (the largest prime ≤ 109 ).

deliberately disjoint implementation, which shares no code with the sieve above: it uses handwritten 128-bit modular exponentiation in place of GMP, and it factors p − 1 by a segmented
sieve of factors over the shifted interval rather than by per-prime trial division, so that an error
in either implementation is unlikely to be mirrored in the other. Both were first checked against
sympy at 106 , where all three routes agree exactly. At 109 the independent implementation
reproduces the published census in every quantity: 50,847,531 primes, 19,016,617 Artin primes,

9. Residue conditioning

fixed? We treat what follows as an exploratory conditioning diagnostic; it does not answer
that question quantitatively. We condition on the joint residue class of (pn , pn+1 ) to modulus

independent observations within each cell, which is not established for this serially structured
deterministic census. The paper does not specify a numerical model for the omitted channels

global figure. The remaining association is small in that metric but is not established to vanish.

not constitute a complete decomposition or an independent quantitative verification of the
Hardy–Littlewood-based explanation of the consecutive-prime bias.
10. The intermediate link: repulsion of ω(p − 1)

consecutive primes repel modulo every small q, then q | pn − 1 makes q | pn+1 − 1 less likely
than average, and summing over q one predicts a negative correlation between ω(pn − 1) and
ω(pn+1 − 1).

The sign reversal is where Theorem 6 becomes visible statistically. For every pair, triplecompleted or not, the primes split into two halves according to the sign of χd (p), d = sqf(ab):
on the half with χd (p) = −1 the values χa (p) and χb (p) are forced opposite, so joint Artin status

the conditional joint density on the complementary half is 0.299.

exclusion — every one of the 66 pairs has it, on exactly half the primes — but the identity of the
barring character χd , and in particular how its conductor interacts with the common cause p − 1.
Among the fifteen triple-completed pairs, d = sqf(ab) takes the values {2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 10, 15, 21},

instead of being diluted by it; we do not have a proof of this and record it as an observation.

12. Decomposition: the correlation is the structure of p − 1
Conditioning on ω(p − 1) alone — the crude size of the factorisation — removes barely
half of the correlation. This is worth emphasising because ω is the statistic through which
the consecutive-prime papers routed the mechanism, and it is genuinely insufficient here: two
primes with the same ω(p − 1) can present very different Artin conditions (it matters which
primes divide p − 1, and with what 2-adic valuation, not how many). The fine signature —
2-adic valuation capped at 4, the exact set of primes ≤ 13 dividing p − 1, and the count of larger
prime factors capped at 3 — is the information the Artin criterion actually consults for small
bases, and conditioning on it removes 95% of the mean correlation (Table 6). That the fine
structure of p − 1, rather than its coarse size, is what governs Artin behaviour is in the same
spirit as the refined order-distribution conjectures of [19],
 which pass from the Artin condition
ordp (a) = p − 1 to the distribution of ω (p − 1)/ordp (a) — finer information about the index
than its mere vanishing, just as our signature is finer information about p − 1 than ω(p − 1).
We regard this as the main statistical finding: cross-base Artin correlation at a single prime
is, to first order, not an interaction between the bases at all. It is a common-cause effect of the
shared integer p − 1. Any model that treats Aa and Ab as conditionally independent given the
small-prime structure of p − 1 reproduces 95% of the observed association.

15. Computational content: a measured negative
The correlations above are statistically overwhelming: δ = −0.01414 with Pearson 10,167 on
one degree of freedom, and individual cross-base values of ϕ/σ0 up to 476. A natural question,
and the one that motivates this section, is whether such a correlation is computationally worth
anything: could it predict generator availability, or yield a faster algorithm? We answer by
measurement, and the answer is no. We record the measurement because the negative is
informative and because the same protocol applies to any claimed predictive advantage in this
area.
The right baseline. A correlation can look large against a naive reference and carry no usable
information once the elementary structure is taken into account. For consecutive primes the
elementary structure is the joint residue pair : a practitioner asking
  whether a is a primitive
root mod p already knows p mod 4d, and the Legendre symbol ap is a function of it. The
correct baseline for a predictive claim is therefore not the marginal density of Art but the
conditional distribution given the joint residues. We measure the cross-entropy of predicting
Artn+1 under three nested models, on all 50,847,530 consecutive pairs below 109 .
The correlation’s own predictive edge, beyond the elementary baseline, is of order 10−5 bits.
It is not usable, and no predictor built on it can beat one built on the residues. One caution for
the reader who repeats this: conditioning on the gap class g mod 40 alone produces a spurious
gain of about 0.02 nats, because g mod 40 does not determine the individual residues pn mod 40
and pn+1 mod 40; the apparent signal is residue information re-appearing. The joint residues
are the correct baseline, and against them the effect is negligible.

What would change the verdict. This is a negative assessment of the evidence, not an
impossibility theorem. The conditioning above does not prove exact conditional independence,
and the verdict would change if: (i) a repeated workload existed in which many compatible
primes or candidate bases are tested and the required labels are already available; (ii) a new
deterministic exclusion were found that rules out a case the character deductions do not
already rule out; (iii) an actionable decision followed from the correlation — skipping a soundly
excluded candidate, or reordering costly tests — with net cost falling after factorisation, feature
acquisition and bookkeeping; and (iv) correctness were preserved, since a predictor may order
tests but cannot replace certification. None of these holds for the correlations studied here.
The results are best understood as describing arithmetic structure, and as identifying exactly
which elementary structure the observed correlations are made of.
16. Discussion
What is new here. The individual ingredients are classical: quadratic reciprocity, the
quadratic-residue obstruction to primitive roots, the consecutive-prime bias of [14], and the
Kummer-degree density computations of [24, 23]. The contributions of this paper are: (i) the
observation that these combine into deterministic exclusion laws on both axes — a gap law for
consecutive primes at base 10 (Theorem 2) and a same-prime pair law with a triple corollary
(Theorem 6, Theorem 7) — each elementary, but none of which we have found stated in this
form; (ii) a measurement, at the scale of 109 primes, of the consecutive-prime Artin correlation,
its gap profile, and the ω repulsion, together with the cross-base correlations ϕ(a, b) and their
joint-density comparison; (iii) an elementary decomposition showing that both correlations
are, to 93–97% and 95% respectively, the same arithmetic structure — the residues of p
and the factorisation of p − 1 — rather than an interaction; and (iv) a measured audit of
the computational content of those correlations, which is nil (§15). The exclusion laws are
immediate applications of standard quadratic reciprocity; their simplicity is a feature, not a
limitation, and the same is true of the audit.
Relation to runs of Artin primes. Pollack [17] proved (under GRH) that for every nonsquare
g ̸= −1 and every m there are infinitely many runs of m consecutive primes each having g as a
primitive root. Baker and Pollack [2] proved unconditionally that if Q contains at least exp(Cm)
multiplicatively independent primes, then infinitely many runs of m consecutive primes each
have some element of Q as a primitive root, with the primes lying in a bounded-length interval.
Our results add an arithmetic constraint to the base-specific version: for base 10, no run of
all-Artin consecutive primes can cross a gap ≡ 20 (mod 40). We have not inferred longer-run
frequencies from the pair statistic, and we emphasise that the existence results above concern
rare favourable configurations rather than the typical behaviour measured here.
Relation to the Kummer-degree theory. The pointwise obstruction of Theorem 6 and the
Kummer-degree heuristic of §14 are both governed by the same quadratic class sqf(ab): in the
first it appears as a congruence barring half the primes, in the second as a member of the group
of quadratic classes that can reduce a degree. We do not claim these are the same phenomenon.
The product class belongs to that group for every pair, whereas it lowers a degree only when
its conductor divides the relevant cyclotomic level, and §14 exhibits pairs separating the two.
The agreement of the Matthews–Moree–Stevenhagen densities with measurement to 0.012%
mean relative error is a check of the density theory at this scale, not a new derivation of it.

Future work. Four extensions suggest themselves. (1) Other bases: the same pipeline with
a = 2, 3, 5, 6, 7 would test the conductor prediction after Corollary 3 (conductor 8 for a = 2, so
exclusion classes mod 8; conductor 24 for a = 6). (2) Scale: extending the consecutive census to

17. Open questions
The Hardy–Littlewood-based framework of [14] makes the residue transition frequencies of
consecutive primes quantitatively predictable, and every channel in our conditioning is built
from such frequencies. This motivates the following open questions.

Research question (residue completeness). Does there exist, for every ε > 0, a
